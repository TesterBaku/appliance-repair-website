How Long Do Appliances Actually Last in Los Angeles County?

Home › Blog › Appliance Lifespan in Los Angeles County

Your dishwasher in Whittier, or the washer in a Long Beach apartment building, might already be older than the "10-year rule" suggests it should be. Los Angeles County's housing is genuinely old by Southern California standards: the median home here was built in 1966. That changes how the lifespan numbers everyone repeats, the ones we published for Orange County too, actually apply here. The U.S. Department of Energy and Consumer Reports both put a number on how long a refrigerator, washer or dishwasher lasts, and they disagree with each other by about four years.

LA County's Older Housing Stock, By the Numbers

The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey 2020-2024 five-year estimates put Los Angeles County's median year of construction at 1966. Seventy percent of the county's housing (70.3%) was built before 1980, and 42.6% was built before 1960. That's a meaningfully older housing stock than the county just south of it. Orange County's median year built is 1977, with 56.8% of housing pre-1980 and only 16.6% pre-1960.

Housing age and occupancy, Los Angeles County vs. Orange County. Source: U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020-2024 5-year estimates
Metric Los Angeles County Orange County
Median year built 1966 1977
Housing built before 1980 70.3% 56.8%
Housing built before 1960 42.6% 16.6%
Renter-occupied households 54.1% 43.6%
Housing in 5+ unit buildings 36.0% 26.1%

Figures are from the American Community Survey 2020-2024 5-year estimates, the most recent vintage available. They describe the county's housing stock overall, not any individual appliance's age.

None of this means an appliance's internal parts age faster because the house around it is older; a compressor doesn't know how old the walls are. It does mean a lot of LA County kitchens and laundry rooms were built around appliances that no longer exist. Our technicians run into this regularly: a refrigerator opening sized for an older, narrower unit, or a laundry closet wired before today's larger dryers became standard. When something breaks in one of these spaces, the repair-or-replace decision is often about the space as much as the appliance itself.

Pasadena and the County's Oldest Kitchens

Some LA County cities skew even older than the countywide average. Pasadena is a clear example: the Census Bureau's 2024 one-year estimate puts roughly a quarter of Pasadena's housing, 26.5%, as built in 1939 or earlier. That single-year estimate carries a wider margin of error than the five-year county figures above (roughly 12% relative), so treat it as directional rather than exact.

Homes that old have usually been through at least one full kitchen remodel by now, and in Pasadena those tend to land on premium built-ins. It's a city where we work almost exclusively with brands like Sub-Zero, Wolf, and Miele rather than the full household lineup. See our Pasadena appliance repair page for what that looks like in practice.

Renters, Multi-Unit Buildings, and How Appliances Get Treated

Housing age is half the local picture. Occupancy type is the other half. LA County's 54.1% renter share and 36.0% share of housing in 5+ unit buildings, both shown in the table above, run well ahead of Orange County's 43.6% and 26.1%.

In our experience, that occupancy split changes how appliances get treated. In an owner-occupied kitchen, a stove that still works sometimes gets replaced anyway for a nicer finish. In a rental, an appliance that still runs almost never gets swapped voluntarily; it gets serviced until it can't be serviced anymore, and it's usually the landlord making that call, not the tenant. Appliances we see failing in LA County's larger rental buildings have frequently run closer to the end of their actual service life than ones replaced early elsewhere.

How Long Should an Appliance Actually Last? DOE's Numbers

Set the housing aside for a moment: how long is an appliance actually built to last? The most rigorous public answer comes from the U.S. Department of Energy, since service life is a required input to the cost-benefit analysis behind every federal efficiency-standards rulemaking. DOE's 2023 and 2024 rulemakings for major residential appliance categories each include a calculated mean service life, built from a survival-curve fit to real shipment and housing-survey data, not a marketing estimate.

DOE calculated mean service life by appliance type. Source: U.S. Department of Energy efficiency-standards rulemakings, 2023-2024
Appliance DOE mean service life
Refrigerator (standard-size) 14.3 years
Freezer (standard) 18.4 years
Freezer (compact) 11.3 years
Clothes washer 13.4 years
Clothes dryer 14 years
Dishwasher 15.2 years
Cooking products (gas)* 14.5 years
Cooking products (electric)* 16.8 years
Microwave 10.78 years

*Cooking-product figures are the exception in DOE's dataset. DOE built these from trade-association (AHAM) input rather than its own independent survival analysis of shipment and housing-survey data the way it did for the other categories, so treat them as softer numbers than the rest of the table. DOE's 14.3-year figure covers standard-size refrigerators and refrigerator-freezers; compact refrigerators are a separate class at 8.8 years.

These are actuarial figures: the age by which roughly half of comparable units get retired, not the age at which any single appliance is guaranteed to fail. A dishwasher at year 10 under DOE's model is roughly two-thirds of the way through its expected service life, not near the end of it.

Why Consumer Reports Gives You a Different Number

Consumer Reports, the other source most homeowners have actually heard of, publishes noticeably shorter numbers: about 10 years for a refrigerator, about 10 years for a dishwasher, "at least a decade" for a washer, and "a decade or so" for a dryer. (Consumer Reports has also published a second, inconsistent dryer figure of 13 years elsewhere on its own site. We're using its "decade or so" framing here and flagging the inconsistency rather than picking whichever number is more convenient.)

That's a real gap from DOE's 14 to 15 year figures for the same categories, and it's worth explaining rather than picking a side. DOE's numbers come from fitting a survival curve to actual shipment and housing-survey data: the mean age at which real units are retired across the installed base, not the halfway point, since a minority of units outlast the typical one by years. Consumer Reports leans more on manufacturer specifications and member-reported replacement habits, closer to "when people say they replace it" than "when the average unit statistically fails." Neither source is wrong; they're measuring different things. For someone deciding whether to repair a 10-year-old dishwasher, DOE would say you're not even at the halfway point yet, while the popular "10 years and done" framing would say you're already there.

The practical takeaway: if your appliance is under 10 years old, DOE's data says you're likely closer to the front half of its expected life than the back half, even if a "rule of thumb" you've heard elsewhere says otherwise. That's worth knowing before you replace something that's still fixable.

Premium Appliances in Los Angeles County

For premium brands, both sources above understate what the manufacturers themselves claim, and LA County has one of the highest concentrations of premium kitchens we service. Sub-Zero describes its refrigeration as built for about 20 years of average use, and Wolf states the same for its cooking products. Miele tests its washing machines and dishwashers to a 10,000-hour standard, which it describes as roughly 20 years of household use. Thermador's published warranty on cooktops and rangetops runs 2 years of full coverage, parts-only from years 2 through 5, and a limited lifetime warranty against rust-through on stainless steel, a structure that signals how long the company expects the surface itself to outlast the mechanical warranty. Terms differ by product line, so check the warranty for your specific model rather than assuming it matches.

We service premium builds across the county, with four LA County cities where we work premium brands only, not the full household lineup: Beverly Hills, Santa Monica, Pasadena, and Manhattan Beach.
