The Real Cost of Skipping Appliance Maintenance in Los Angeles County

Home › Blog › Cost of Skipping Appliance Maintenance in Los Angeles County

A landlord in Norwalk used to be able to let a fridge run a little warm for a few weeks before dealing with it. As of January 1, 2026, that changed for a lot of Los Angeles County leases: California's Assembly Bill 628 (AB 628) makes a working stove and refrigerator part of what counts as a habitable rental. That turns skipped maintenance from a pure cost question into a legal-exposure one too, and it raises a second question nobody was really asking before: who actually pays when the upkeep gets skipped in the first place?

What AB 628 Changes About Skipping Maintenance

This is general information, not legal advice. Every lease and every dispute turns on its own facts. If you're a landlord or tenant in Los Angeles County dealing with a specific disagreement over an appliance, a recall, or lease terms, talk to a California landlord-tenant attorney or your local legal aid office. We repair appliances for a living; we don't practice law.

We covered the mechanics of California's AB 628 in detail in our companion piece on LA County's new rental appliance law, so we won't repeat the statute language here. The short version: for a lease entered into, amended, or extended on or after January 1, 2026, Civil Code Section 1941.1 now requires a landlord to keep the stove and refrigerator in good working order, and a manufacturer or public-entity recall on either one starts a 30-day repair-or-replace clock.

What that means for maintenance is simple, but worth spelling out plainly. A stove or fridge that's been neglected for years is more likely to fail outright, and on a covered lease that failure isn't just an inconvenience to get to on the landlord's own schedule anymore; it's the exact condition the statute now targets. Routine maintenance doesn't guarantee a landlord never trips that wire. A compressor can fail on a well-maintained unit just as easily as a neglected one, sometimes. But skipping the basic upkeep that's cheap and well understood, coil cleaning, vent clearing, a working door seal, stacks the odds toward exactly the kind of breakdown AB 628 was written to address.

Who Actually Pays When Maintenance Gets Skipped

Maintenance costs money either way. Someone has to pay for the coil brush, the vent cleaning, the fill-hose swap, whether that's the person living in the unit or the person who owns it. In an owner-occupied home, that's the same person, so the incentive to stay ahead of it is straightforward. In a rental, it usually isn't.

Los Angeles County has a lot more renters than the county just south of it. The Census Bureau's American Community Survey (2020-2024 five-year estimates) puts 54.1% of LA County households in renter-occupied housing, against 43.6% in Orange County, a gap we cover in more depth alongside the county's older housing stock in our LA County appliance lifespan article.

In our experience, that split changes who notices a maintenance gap first and how fast anything gets done about it. A homeowner who lets refrigerator coils go a couple of years without cleaning is really only costing themselves a few dollars a month on the electric bill. A landlord who skips the same task on a rental unit is looking at the same slow wear, except it plays out differently for each side.

The tenant is the one paying the higher electric bill in the meantime. The landlord is the one who eventually pays for the early compressor call once the unit gives out. Neither side has full visibility into what the other is dealing with day to day, which is part of why a law like AB 628 exists in the first place.

The Best-Documented Preventable Failure: Dryer Fires

Of everything we checked for this article, the dryer-fire numbers are the best documented, and it isn't close. The National Fire Protection Association tracked an estimated 13,820 home structure fires a year involving clothes dryers, a 2014-2018 average, with 7 civilian deaths, 344 injuries, and $233 million in direct property damage annually. Failure to clean was the single leading factor, responsible for 32% of those fires, and dust, fiber, or lint was the first item ignited in 27% of them. The U.S. Fire Administration's own topical reporting on residential dryer fires, built from a separate federal dataset, backs up the same failure-to-clean pattern independently of NFPA's numbers.

That risk doesn't sit evenly across LA County's housing stock, either. A single-family home usually has a short, straight vent run to an exterior wall. A dryer in one of the county's many multi-unit buildings, 36.0% of LA County housing sits in buildings with five or more units per that same Census data, often vents through a longer run that's harder to inspect and shared with the building's structure.

The single most serious thing on this page: vent cleaning is cheap and takes a technician fifteen minutes to half an hour. Skipping it is the leading documented cause of a fire risk that kills people. Nothing else in this article carries stakes that high.

What PNNL Actually Recommends for Refrigerator Coils

Refrigerator coils are a smaller-stakes problem, but a real one. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory's Building America Solution Center recommends cleaning a refrigerator's condenser coils every 6 to 12 months, and says doing so regularly improves the refrigerator's efficiency, performance, and lifespan. We went looking for a second, independently collected source to put a specific percentage on how much that actually raises an energy bill, and couldn't find one we'd stand behind, so we're not going to repeat a number here we can't back up.

The advice itself doesn't need a second source to be worth following. Cleaning coils costs nothing beyond a vacuum and a coil brush, takes about fifteen minutes, and is PNNL's own stated guidance rather than a marketing claim from us or anyone else. On a rental where AB 628 now applies, it's also one of the cheapest things a landlord can do to keep a refrigerator from being the appliance that fails first.

What Consumer Reports' Data Says About Refrigerator Reliability

How often does a refrigerator actually need service in the first place, maintained or not? Consumer Reports' 2026 survey update, based on 71,534 refrigerators purchased since 2015, found that 49% had experienced some kind of problem within the first five years. That's a Consumer Reports member-reported figure, and we didn't find it independently corroborated by a second source this time around, so we're presenting it as Consumer Reports' own finding rather than a settled industry consensus.

It's still the largest sample size of any refrigerator reliability data we could locate, and it's a useful gut check against the "ten years and done" assumption a lot of homeowners carry into a repair-or-replace decision. Half of that 49% traces to icemaker and dispenser issues rather than the sealed system itself, which is one more reason coil cleaning and basic upkeep matter: the compressor is the expensive part to replace, and it's also the part that benefits most from not being run harder than it has to be.

What Maintenance Can't Prevent

Maintenance shifts odds; it doesn't rewrite them. A control board fails on its own schedule regardless of how clean the coils are. A compressor that's near the end of its expected service life, which the U.S. Department of Energy put at roughly 14.3 years for a standard refrigerator in its 2024 efficiency-standards rulemaking, will eventually go even on a unit that's been maintained perfectly. A manufacturer defect shows up in year one no matter what anyone does afterward, and no amount of coil cleaning changes that.

  • Control board failures. These fail at rates largely uncorrelated with cleaning or upkeep, and rise with age regardless of maintenance history.
  • Manufacturer defects. A year-one failure is almost always a factory defect, not a maintenance gap; that's what the warranty is for.
  • Compressor failure at end of expected life. Maintenance can push the expected failure year out, but it doesn't make a refrigerator immortal.

What routine maintenance actually buys is a lower chance of the preventable failures: the dryer-vent fire, the compressor that burns out early because it's been fighting dirty coils for years, the fill hose that finally bursts because nobody replaced it on schedule. On a rental where AB 628 now applies, it also buys the landlord a cleaner record if a habitability question ever comes up, though maintenance alone isn't a substitute for actually fixing something once it breaks.

The practical takeaway: cleaning condenser coils costs a vacuum, a coil brush, and about fifteen minutes twice a year.
