KitchenAid Built-In Refrigerator Not Cooling: Temperature Sensor and Control Board Problems

Home › Blog › KitchenAid Refrigerator: Sensor and Control Board

Your KitchenAid built-in stops holding temperature, a technician comes out, and somewhere in that visit you hear the words "high voltage" and "temperature sensor." By the time you're describing the fix to a neighbor or looking up the part online, the two phrases have merged into one: a "high voltage temperature sensor." It's an easy mix-up, and it's also not a real part. We ran into this exact mix-up after a recent KitchenAid built-in refrigerator call in Aliso Viejo, when the way the visit got described afterward didn't match any part number we could find anywhere. Here's what a KitchenAid built-in actually uses to sense and regulate temperature, and how those two very real words ended up glued together.

What Actually Senses Temperature Inside a KitchenAid Built-In

The part actually doing the sensing is called a thermistor, and in the repair trade, "thermistor" and "temperature sensor" get used interchangeably. You'll find guides titled one way that explain the part the other way, because they're the same thing. A KitchenAid built-in typically carries two to four of them: one for the fresh food section, one for the freezer, and often another watching the evaporator coil or the defrost cycle. Each thermistor is a small, low-voltage part. It doesn't switch anything on its own. It just reports a resistance value that changes with temperature, and the control reads that value to decide what to do next.

The High Voltage Board Is Real. It's Not a Sensor.

Here's where the confusion actually starts. "High Voltage Board" is a real OEM part name, not a nickname a technician made up on the spot. Whirlpool sells one under part number W10890094, listed as fitting KitchenAid among other Whirlpool-family brands, and it does exactly what the name suggests: it's the main control board that reads every temperature sensor in the unit and switches the compressor, the fan motors, and the defrost heater on and off. Whirlpool-built refrigerators, KitchenAid included, commonly split control into two boards: a low-voltage board near the dispenser or user interface that runs the display and buttons, and this high voltage board, usually mounted toward the back, that does the actual switching. So a technician explaining a fault might accurately say something like "the sensor's fine, it's the high voltage board" in one breath, using two correct terms for two different parts.

That's likely where the confusion comes from. Say "high voltage board" and "temperature sensor" enough times in the same conversation about the same fridge, and it's easy to see how a homeowner walks away remembering one part instead of two. We looked, and no manufacturer or major parts retailer, not PartSelect, not RepairClinic, not Amazon, lists anything called a "high voltage temperature sensor." It simply doesn't exist as a component. Two real terms just get compressed into one memorable phrase that happens to be wrong.

A Third Real Part: The Bimetal Defrost Thermostat

There's a third temperature-sensing part worth knowing, and it's the one that genuinely does carry line voltage: the bimetal defrost thermostat. Sears PartsDirect calls it the refrigerator defrost sensor, and PartSelect sells it for KitchenAid units among other brands. Unlike the thermistor, it's a mechanical switch, not an electronic sensor feeding a signal to a board. It closes and opens based on the physical temperature of the evaporator coil, and its job is keeping the defrost heater from running once the coil is already cold enough. If any of these three parts actually deserved to be called "high voltage," it would be this one, since it genuinely is.

Telling the Three Faults Apart

Each of these three parts fails in its own recognizable way, and the symptom usually points to which one is at fault before a technician even opens a panel.

1

A Failing Thermistor

The compressor runs nonstop and the unit still can't hold temperature, because the control is reading a false signal and overcompensating. If it's specifically the defrost thermistor, you'll often see heavy frost building up on the evaporator coil instead.

2

A Failing High Voltage Board

The fault tends to look bigger and more sudden. Because one board switches the compressor, the fans, and the defrost heater, a board failure can knock out several of them at once instead of just one system slowly drifting.

3

A Stuck Bimetal Defrost Thermostat

The problem shows up specifically in the defrost cycle: either the heater runs longer than it should, or it doesn't run at all. One pattern melts more than necessary near the coil; the other lets frost build up endlessly until airflow gets blocked.

All three are electrical parts sitting in a cold, sometimes damp environment, and they tend to fail for the same handful of reasons: age, corrosion at the connector, and moisture working into a connection point after years of temperature cycling. None of them are wear items the way a door gasket is. They just eventually corrode or drift out of calibration.

What KitchenAid's Own Alarms Actually Tell You

KitchenAid documents two built-in alerts worth knowing before you call anyone. The Over Temperature alarm triggers when the refrigerator section stays above 48°F, or the freezer stays above 15°F, for 1.5 hours straight. KitchenAid's own fix is a power reset at the breaker. If the alarm comes back after that, its guidance is to schedule service.

A flashing F code gets the same two-step answer from KitchenAid: reset power, and if it returns, schedule service. KitchenAid does not publish a cause for either one, no mention of a bad sensor, no mention of airflow, nothing more specific than "schedule service." We won't invent a cause the manufacturer itself doesn't give you. A technician needs to actually test the sensors, the board, and the seals to find out which one is at fault.

Frequently Asked Questions: KitchenAid Refrigerator Temperature Sensor and Control Board Issues

Is a 'high voltage temperature sensor' a real KitchenAid refrigerator part?

No. No manufacturer or major parts retailer, including PartSelect, RepairClinic, or Amazon, lists a part called a 'high voltage temperature sensor.' What actually exists are two separate real parts with similar-sounding names: the thermistor, which is the low-voltage sensor that measures temperature, and the High Voltage Board, which is the control board that reads the sensor signal and switches the compressor, fans, and defrost heater. Hearing both terms in the same visit and remembering them as one part is an easy mix-up.

What's the difference between the thermistor and the High Voltage Board?

The thermistor is the actual temperature sensor. A KitchenAid built-in typically has two to four of them, covering the fresh food section, the freezer, and often the evaporator or defrost coil, and each one is a small low-voltage part that reports temperature back to the control. The High Voltage Board is a different component entirely. It is sold under that exact name, Whirlpool part W10890094, listed as fitting KitchenAid among other Whirlpool-family brands, and its job is reading every sensor and switching the compressor, the fan motors, and the defrost heater on and off. One part measures temperature. The other acts on what it measures.

What does it mean if my KitchenAid built-in shows an Over Temperature alarm?

KitchenAid's own documentation says this alarm triggers when the refrigerator section stays above 48°F, or the freezer stays above 15°F, for 1.5 hours straight. The manufacturer's recommended first step is a power reset at the breaker, not a specific part diagnosis. If the alarm returns after the reset, KitchenAid's guidance is to schedule service so a technician can find the actual cause, which could be a sensor, the control board, a door seal, or something in the sealed cooling system.

What does a flashing 'F' code mean on a KitchenAid built-in refrigerator?

KitchenAid does not publish what a flashing F code means on its built-in refrigerators. Its documented guidance is the same two steps as the Over Temperature alarm: reset power at the breaker, and if the code returns, schedule service. Because the manufacturer does not disclose a cause, we will not guess at one either. A technician needs to test the unit directly to find which part actually triggered it.
